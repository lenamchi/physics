What electromagnetic waves are

State that all electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that travel with the same speed in vacuum and state the magnitude of this speed.

In the previous chapter you learnt about waves and their properties. This chapter looks at one family of waves: electromagnetic (EM) waves.

An EM wave is made of an electric field and a magnetic field that oscillate at 90° to each other. Both fields are also perpendicular to the direction the wave travels, which is why EM waves are transverse. You can see this in the explorer above: the blue E field and the orange B field wobble at right angles while the wave moves to the right.

Transverse

E and B fields oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel.

Transfer energy

EM waves carry energy from one place to another.

Travel through vacuum

No medium is needed. This is what sets them apart from sound and water waves.

Same speed in vacuum

All EM waves travel at c ≈ 3.0 × 108 m s−1 in vacuum.

Obey v = fλ

Like all waves, they follow the wave equation.

Reflect and refract

They obey the laws of reflection and refraction.

No charge

EM waves are neither positively nor negatively charged, so electric and magnetic fields do not deflect them.

Changing medium

Frequency stays the same. Speed and wavelength change.

Frequency and energy

The higher the frequency, the higher the energy.

The wave equation
v = f λ

v = wave speed (m s−1), f = frequency (Hz), λ = wavelength (m). In vacuum, v = c = 3.0 × 108 m s−1 for every EM wave, so a shorter wavelength always means a higher frequency.

Entering glass or water: the frequency is set by the source, so it stays the same. The wave slows down, so by v = fλ its wavelength gets shorter.

The electromagnetic spectrum

Describe the main components of the electromagnetic spectrum.

EM waves come in a continuous range of wavelengths (and frequencies), so together they form a spectrum. We name each part of the spectrum by its wavelength or frequency range. The boundaries between regions are approximate; neighbouring regions overlap slightly.

Effects of absorbing EM waves

Describe the effects of absorbing electromagnetic waves, e.g. heating, ionisation and damage to living cells and tissue.

HeatingMicrowaves · Infra-red

When body tissue absorbs microwaves, water molecules are made to vibrate vigorously. This raises the temperature of the tissue and can cause internal burns. Infra-red is absorbed by the skin and heats it.

Microwaves absorbed→water molecules vibrate→heating
IonisationUltra-violet · X-rays · Gamma

Ionisation is the removal (or addition) of electrons from atoms or molecules, turning them into charged ions. High-frequency EM waves carry enough energy to do this.

Ionisation→damages biological molecules (DNA)→cell death, mutations, cancer, deformed foetus

The higher the frequency, the more energy the wave carries, and the more harmful it can be. That same damage is useful when it is controlled: gamma rays are aimed at tumours in radiotherapy to kill cancer cells, and UV kills bacteria when sterilising equipment.

Extra note

Radio waves vs microwaves in satellite communication. Both are useful; the difference is the distance between the two communication points.

  • Radio waves have less energy and are often reflected or dissipated by the ionosphere.
  • Microwaves have more energy and pass through the ionosphere.

Microwaves are better for communicating with geostationary satellites that are a long distance away (about 36 000 km above Earth), e.g. Wi-Fi, GPS devices, mobile phones and satellite TV signals.

Radio waves are better for communicating with low-orbiting satellites, e.g. broadcasting radio programmes and television stations.
