Electric charge

(a) State that there are positive and negative charges and that charge is measured in coulombs. (b) State that unlike charges attract and like charges repel.

As a recap of what you learnt in Chemistry, matter is made up of atoms. Each atom has a nucleus of protons and neutrons, with electrons moving around it.

The coulomb (C) is the SI unit of electric charge. The charge on one electron is tiny: −1.6 × 10−19 C.

Electric fields

(c) Describe an electric field as a region in which an electric charge experiences a force. (d) Draw the electric field of an isolated point charge and recall that the direction of the field lines gives the direction of the force acting on a positive test charge. (e) Draw the electric field pattern between two isolated point charges.

An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences an electric force.

An electric force is the attractive or repulsive force that charges exert on one another. It acts without the charges touching.

We draw electric field lines, with arrowheads, to show a field. The direction of an electric field is the direction of the force that would act on a small positive charge placed in that field. A negative charge is pushed the opposite way.

Rules for drawing field lines
  1. Arrows point away from a positive charge and towards a negative charge.
  2. Lines are closer together where the field (and force) is stronger.
  3. Field lines never cross.

Also: lines start and end at right angles to the surface of a charged sphere, and should be evenly spaced around a single charge.

Memory aid · the three rules

Arrows go out of + and into − · Closer lines = stronger field · Never cross. Field lines, like Ah Chong, never cross where they shouldn't.

Charging and discharging

(f) Show understanding that electrostatic charging by rubbing involves a transfer of electrons. (g) Describe experiments to show electrostatic charging by induction.

We can charge both insulators and conductors, but the method is different for each.

Charging by friction (insulators only)
Glass rod rubbed with silk

When two insulators are rubbed together, electrons are transferred from one to the other. Some atoms hold their electrons less tightly than others.

Glass and silk: electrons move from the glass rod to the silk. The silk becomes negative and the glass rod positive. The electrons cannot move in an insulator, so they stay on the surface where it was rubbed.

You do not need to memorise which material becomes which: fur (+) / ebonite (−), glass (+) / silk (−), hair (+) / rubber balloon (−), Perspex (+) / wool (−), wool (+) / polythene (−).

Why can't you charge a metal rod held in your hand by rubbing? It is a conductor: the charge flows away through your hand and body to earth.
Charging by induction (conductors) · no contact

One conductor (positive rod):

  1. Bring the charged rod near, but not touching. Electrons are attracted to the near end, as unlike charges attract. The far end is left positive.
  2. With the rod in place, earth the conductor (touch it). Electrons flow from earth into the conductor, neutralising the positive end.
  3. Remove the earth (take your hand away) while the rod is still there.
  4. Remove the rod. The extra electrons spread evenly. The conductor is now negative: opposite to the rod.

Never Eat Uncle's Rendang (before it's ready)

Near · Earth · Unearth · Remove rod. The rod is like the auntie who chopes the kopitiam table: first to come, last to go.

Activity · induction simulator

Your mission: leave the metal sphere charged, using the rod but without touching it. Try the steps in different orders and watch the electrons.

Rod

Two conductors (spheres A and B touching)
  1. Place two metal spheres on insulating stands so they touch.
  2. Bring a negatively charged rod near (not touching) sphere A. Electrons in both spheres are repelled to the far side of B. A is left positive, B negative.
  3. Move B away so the spheres no longer touch (rod still in place).
  4. Remove the rod. The charges spread evenly: A is positive, B is negative, with equal amounts of opposite charge.
Earthing

Earthing connects an object to the ground, which can supply or accept huge numbers of electrons.

Positive conductor: electrons flow from earth into it. Negative conductor: the extra electrons flow to earth. Either way it ends up neutral.

Discharging an insulator · heating

Heat the charged insulator. The intense heat ionises the surrounding air. The ions are attracted to the charged insulator and neutralise its excess charges.

Thinking question 1: what if the rod is removed before the hand?

The sphere ends up neutral. While it is still earthed, the electrons that were held by the rod are no longer attracted, so they flow back to earth. Try it in the simulator.

Thinking question 2: does the position of earthing matter?

No. The conductor is one connected piece of metal, so electrons can flow in from earth wherever you touch it, even near the rod. The result is the same.

Hazards and applications

(h) Describe examples where electrostatic charging may be a potential hazard. (i) Describe the use of electrostatic charging in an electrostatic precipitator, and apply the use of electrostatic charging to new situations.

HazardLightning
Friction between water and air molecules charges thunderclouds→negative charge at the cloud bottom→repels electrons: ground below becomes positive→air is ionised, giving a conducting path→electrons rush to earth: lightning

Prevention: lightning conductor. Sharp metal spikes on top of a building, joined by copper strips to a metal plate in the ground. The charged cloud induces charge on the spikes, and the conductor gives a steady, safe discharge path to earth, so a sudden strike is less likely.

HazardElectrostatic discharge
Friction builds up charge→charge gets large→sudden discharge: a spark→fire, explosion or damaged electronics

A spark near fuel vapour, flour dust or flammable gas can cause a fire or explosion.

ObjectHazardPrevention
Fuel tanker truckFriction between the road and the rotating tyres builds up charge. A sudden discharge may cause sparks and ignite the flammable load.A metal chain hangs from the back to the ground, giving an earthing path for the excess charge.
Aircraft in flightFriction with air and ice crystals charges the aircraft.Discharge rods on the wings let the charge leak away into the surrounding air.
Refuelling an aircraftFuel flowing through the hose is charged by friction; a spark could ignite the fuel.A wire connects the aircraft to earth before refuelling.
Application · electrostatic precipitator

Removes smoke, dust and droplets from waste gases in incineration plants, power stations, chemical plants and factories.

  1. Gas with unwanted particles passes negatively charged wires.
  2. The particles become negatively charged.
  3. They pass positively charged (or earthed) metal plates, which attract them, as unlike charges attract.
  4. Gas leaving the chimney is free of the particles. The plates are knocked so the dust falls off to be collected.
Application · electrostatic spray painting
  1. Paint droplets become positively charged by friction as they leave the nozzle.
  2. Like charges repel, so the droplets spread out into a fine, even spray.
  3. As the positive droplets approach the car body, free electrons in the metal are attracted to its surface, which attracts the droplets onto the target, even round corners.
  4. Less paint is wasted and the coat is more even.

Also: laser printers and photocopiers use a charged drum to pick up toner powder.

Q1(a)(i)
  1. Bring the positively charged rod near to, but not touching, the metal plate.
  2. With the rod held in place, touch the lead connected to earth on the metal plate.
  3. Remove the lead.
  4. Then remove the rod.
Q1(a)(ii)

Step 1: when the charged rod is brought near the metal plate, electrons in the plate are attracted by the positively charged rod and move to the left side of the plate, leaving the right side with excess positive charge, as unlike charges attract.

Step 2: when the metal plate is connected to earth, electrons flow from the ground into the plate to neutralise the positive charges on the right side of the plate, as unlike charges attract.

Steps 3 and 4: when the lead and then the charged rod are removed, the excess negative charges on the left side of the plate spread themselves evenly over the plate, as like charges repel. The plate is negatively charged.

Q1(b)(i)

Where light strikes, the drum surface becomes conducting and is connected to the earthed metal inside. Electrons flow from earth to neutralise the positive charge, so these regions lose their charge.

Q1(b)(ii)

1. The toner is positively charged, and like charges repel.

2. The conducting regions are uncharged (and earthed), so they do not repel the toner; the charged toner induces an opposite charge on them and is attracted to them.

Handout examples
Example 1

(a) The charged conductor has to be suspended on a string so that the charges on it are not conducted away, as the string is an electrical insulator.

(b) When the negatively-charged conductor is brought near the metal plate, the negative charges on the plate are repelled, as like charges repel. Since the plate is connected to earth, the negative charges are transferred to the ground. The momentary flow of charge to the ground causes the ammeter to show a deflection.

Example 2

(a) Friction between the floor and the shoes of the students caused electric charges to build up on the students. When they touch, a sudden discharge occurs, leading to a static shock.

(b) Dragging their feet increases the contact and friction between the floor and the students' shoes. This leads to more charging by friction.

(c) The likelihood would increase, as the carpet has an uneven surface and increases the surface area in contact with the shoes, increasing the friction.
